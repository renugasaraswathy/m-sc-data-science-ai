Gradient Descent and Momentum¶

Key Concepts¶

1. What is Optimisation?¶

The goal of training¶

  • Training aims to minimise the loss. Optimisation = finding the weights and biases that make the loss as small as possible. The optimiser explores combinations of parameters, trying to move the model into a region where it performs well.

The loss landscape¶

  • With thousands or millions of parameters, the loss is a high-dimensional surface with valleys, hills, plateaus, ridges and saddle points.
  • There can be several local minima and one global minimum; near a saddle point the surface is almost flat.
  • ★ Optimisation finds a good enough valley and navigates the landscape efficiently, avoiding the regions that slow learning.
  • Why it's hard: the surface is high-dimensional, gradients can be noisy or inconsistent, some regions are nearly flat and others very steep, and a poor learning rate can make training slow or unstable.

What the optimiser controls¶

Aspect Tool
Step size Learning rate
Directional smoothing Momentum
Per-parameter adjustment Adaptive optimisers (RMSProp, Adam)
Stability Gradient clipping, normalisation
  • ★ Together these decide convergence speed, final accuracy and training stability. Optimisation is more than computing the gradients.

2. Gradient Descent¶

Intuition¶

  • Imagine standing on a curved landscape defined by the loss, with the parameter as a ball. Gradient descent: look at the slope and step downhill.
    • Slope negative (loss rises to the left) → move right.
    • Slope positive (loss rises to the right) → move left.
    • Slope ≈ flat → near a minimum.
  • The gradient points in the direction the loss grows fastest (uphill), so the optimiser moves in the opposite direction.

Update rule¶

θ_new = θ_old − η · ∇L

  • θ = the weight being updated, η = learning rate, ∇L = gradient (the slope at the current point).
  • The gradient gives the direction; the learning rate controls how big a step. Every parameter follows the same rule.

Learning rate¶

Learning rate Effect
Too small Tiny steps, training extremely slow
Too large Overshoots the minimum or diverges (loss increases)
Well chosen Smooth, efficient progress downhill
  • Real networks have millions of dimensions: the gradient is a vector with a slope for every weight and bias, but the logic is unchanged: take a step that reduces the loss the most.

Worked check: L(θ) = (θ − 3)²¶

  • Gradient ∇L = 2(θ − 3), start at θ = 0.
Step η = 0.1 η = 1.1
Start θ = 0, gradient −6 θ = 0, gradient −6
After 1 update θ = 0.6 (gradient −4.8) θ = 6.6 (gradient +7.2)
After 2 updates θ = 1.08 (steadily approaching 3) θ = −1.32 (overshooting more each time)
  • η = 0.1 creeps toward the minimum at 3; η = 1.1 jumps past it and the distance from 3 keeps growing (3 → 3.6 → 4.3 → …): divergence.

Practical points from the live class¶

  • Where this sits: backpropagation gives the gradient of the loss for every weight and bias; optimisation is the step that uses those gradients to update them. Then the next batch uses the new weights, the loss and gradients are recomputed, and the loop repeats.
  • The loss is one number, the gradients are per parameter. The loss is computed once for the whole batch; each parameter then gets its own partial derivative (its share of the blame), e.g. W1 ← W1 − η · ∂E/∂W1, W2 ← W2 − η · ∂E/∂W2. It is a joint optimisation: all parameters are updated together and do not counter each other.
  • Only the sign matters for direction: positive gradient → decrease the parameter, negative gradient → increase it. The actual weight value can be anything (−2, +10).
  • η is a scaling factor on the gradient (typical values 0.1, 0.01, 10⁻³), not a "rate" in the physics sense; it is the size of the change in θ per update.
  • Reaching a minimum: the slope is ≈ 0, so θ_new ≈ θ_old and updates stall. It is normal to explore a little uphill to check it is a real minimum. You often end in a good local minimum, and that is fine; you never see the true loss curve, only the path your training traces.
  • When to stop (in practice): real loss surfaces are not smooth, and some weights may want to move in opposite directions near the end. Teams stop when the loss stops improving or when business metrics (precision, recall, ...) are good enough.
  • One data point is enough for a gradient: the loss has a fixed functional form (e.g. (y − ŷ)² or −log ŷ), so you can differentiate it for a single sample. That is why SGD (batch of 1) works.

3. Variants of Gradient Descent¶

Why variants exist¶

  • With millions of samples, computing one gradient over the whole dataset is slow, expensive, memory-heavy and hard to scale. The variants differ only in how much data each update uses.

    A["Batch GD<br/>entire dataset<br/>per update"] --> D["Trade-off:<br/>stability vs speed"]
    B["SGD<br/>1 sample<br/>per update"] --> D
    C["Mini-batch SGD<br/>small batch<br/>per update"] --> D
Batch GD Stochastic GD (SGD) Mini-batch GD
Data per update Entire dataset One sample Small batch (typically 32–512)
Update quality Stable, smooth, correct direction Very noisy, loss bounces around Stable enough, with useful noise
Speed / cost Very slow, memory-heavy, poor GPU use Very fast per update Fast; uses GPU parallelism well
Typical use Small datasets or convex problems Online / streaming data; rarely used alone Default for neural networks
  • Noise from SGD isn't always bad: it can help escape saddle points or sharp minima.

Batch size matters¶

Batch size Behaviour
Small (16, 32) Noisier gradients; can improve generalisation and escape bad regions
Large (1024+) Very smooth gradients, but may converge to sharp minima and need learning-rate tuning or warm-up
32–256 Works extremely well for most tasks
  • ★ Batch size is one of the most important hyperparameters. Noise is sometimes exactly what the optimiser needs to keep moving.

Practical points from the live class¶

  • Naming is confusing: "batch gradient descent" means the entire dataset per update. "Mini-batch" means whatever batch size you train with (e.g. 32). When code or slides say SGD, it almost always means mini-batch gradient descent.
  • Mini-batches are formed by random sampling, so re-training can give a slightly different loss curve (e.g. 45 vs 60 epochs to converge). Well-represented batches give smoother curves.
  • Batch GD is the ideal but impractical for large data; pure SGD (1 sample) suits online / real-time learning but gives a very wavy loss.

4. Momentum¶

The problem: zig-zag¶

  • In a narrow valley (steep in one direction, shallow in the one we want), the gradient keeps pointing left and right across the walls. The optimiser wastes time correcting direction and converges slowly.

The idea: velocity¶

  • Momentum keeps a running average of past gradients, like a ball rolling downhill: it keeps going through a gentle patch because it has accumulated speed.
    • Gradients consistently in one direction → the update builds speed.
    • Gradients oscillate → momentum smooths them out.
    • Gradients very small → momentum carries the update forward.

Update rule¶

  • v_t = β · v_{t−1} + (1 − β) · ∇L
  • θ_t = θ_{t−1} − η · v_t
  • β = how much of the previous velocity to keep; typical β = 0.9 (keep 90% of the previous direction). Conceptually: a weighted average of recent gradients.
flowchart LR
    A["Without momentum<br/>zig-zags across the valley"] --> C["Wasted steps,<br/>slow progress"]
    B["With momentum<br/>smoother, more direct path"] --> D["Faster progress along<br/>the valley floor"]

Benefits¶

  • Accelerates convergence, especially in long, narrow valleys.
  • Reduces oscillation in steep dimensions.
  • Helps escape shallow local minima; gives more stable training.
  • Most useful with noisy mini-batch gradients, slow training, or narrow valleys.
  • A standard addition to SGD; Adam includes a version of momentum internally.

Practical points from the live class¶

  • Reading β: β = 0.9 gives 90% weight to history and 10% to the current gradient; 1 − β is the weight on the current gradient. β lies between 0 and 1.
  • Momentum carries across batches: v_{t−1} for the next batch is the v_t from the previous batch, so the velocity keeps accumulating through training.
  • In the update, the velocity replaces the raw gradient (θ ← θ − η · v_t).
  • Momentum helps most in deep valleys and strongly curved regions, and on flat patches.
  • All gradient descent variants share one global learning rate η for every parameter; that is the limitation adaptive optimisers address (next lesson).
